# Product and collision audit

## Read the product, then test the name

For an existing product, inspect the source that owns the experience. Use its product brief, the labels in a core workflow, and available desktop or mobile captures. Label intended future audiences and workflows as scenarios. For a platform that starts in job search, test discovery, comparing or saving opportunities, and application tracking today. Then test a working professional keeping a profile, asking for a referral, or interacting with an employer if those are planned uses.

| Question | Test |
|---|---|
| What does a new user think this does? | Show or say the bare name and ask for a category guess before showing a tagline or explaining the root. For desk review, say what is inferred. |
| Does it sound natural in the product? | Substitute the name into actual high-frequency copy. Try a primary action such as “Ask [Name]” only if that action exists. Check narrow navigation, sign-in, and shared previews where relevant. |
| Does it still fit the planned platform? | Try “Find me on [Name]” or “[Name] for Employers” when those surfaces match the roadmap. Compare the same phrase for each contender and note where a user identity or feature feels too narrow. |
| Can people type it after hearing it? | List plausible pronunciations, then likely spellings and typos for each. With listeners, say the name once without showing it and record first guesses and the sample size. Without listeners, label the results as desk-based. |
| Can people find it after hearing it? | Search the exact form and the most plausible heard spellings both alone and with the category. Record the result and its relevance. A strong incumbent in a nearby category matters more than an unrelated typo match. |
| Can people use it in priority markets? | Check pronunciation, transliteration, meaning and slang, and local search results where relevant. Document the languages and markets actually checked. Escalate unresolved readings to native speakers before committing. |

Search findability and demand capture are different tasks. Favor a distinctive, consistent brand spelling people can retrieve. Use useful pages that address the product's category queries. Avoid giving a keyword-heavy domain extra credit solely for matching a query. ## Check adjacent businesses and marks

1. Search exact, phonetic, spelling, spacing, and root variants. Add the current category and the adjacent categories in the roadmap. Inspect the primary sites of relevant businesses to confirm what they sell and whom they serve.
2. Search official registers for relevant territories. Open records for close results and record status, jurisdiction, owner, and overlapping goods or services. A matching class alone is insufficient to conclude a conflict. Market use can matter even without a record in the registers checked.
3. Distinguish confirmed conflicts with the brief, material concerns that put a name on hold, and ordinary trade-offs. Explain why the degree of similarity and market overlap changes the decision. Link the actual result or record, and describe the limits of preliminary screening.

For example, a hypothetical name could score 10/10 in the spelling script and have no registration record for its exact domain. If a plausible heard spelling leads users to an active company in the same category, that evidence weakens both findability and collision confidence. Keep the candidate conditional or put it on hold depending on the overlap and the alternatives. An exact affordable quote would solve only the domain requirement.
